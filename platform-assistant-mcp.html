Platform Assistant over MCP

A chat assistant that answers questions about the platform's models and data directly, connected over the same MCP layer as the agent connectors work.

5 min read

The problem

Finding an answer about a model, who owns it, what's downstream of a field, when it's next due for review, usually means leaving the platform to dig through a report or ask a colleague.

The approach

Put the answer inside the chat tool people already have open, grounded in the platform's real data over MCP, the same connector pattern as the agent connectors project.

How we judged it

Does it give a real answer when there is one, admit it when there isn't, and decline gracefully when asked to do something it isn't supposed to do?

Real client details, objects and data are confidential. The prototype below uses a fictional bank, Northwind Decisions, and its responses are scripted rather than live.

Framing

Defined as much by what it refuses

Once the agent connectors project had an MCP server exposing the platform's data, a chat assistant was an obvious next step. The easy version of that is a demo: ask it something it's good at, get an impressive answer, move on. The useful version has to survive the other three cases too: a search with no matches, a question about something outside its scope, and someone asking it to take an action it shouldn't take on their behalf.

Approve model_x for me is the case I spent the most time on. The assistant has read access to plenty of data, which makes it tempting to assume it can act on your behalf too. It can't, and it has to say so plainly instead of deflecting or pretending it tried. That one boundary matters more to trust than any amount of accurate search results does.

Outcome

Built, deliberately narrow

The assistant is connected to the platform over MCP and answers real questions about real models and data. Its responses are scripted in the walkthrough above so I could show the shape of the conversation publicly: what it answers, what it deflects, and what it refuses, without exposing an actual bank's models or data.

What I'd take further

  • Which refusals are worth turning into real features. "Approve this for me" should stay out of scope. Some of the others are more borderline and worth building properly instead of declining forever.
  • How far server instructions scale. Teaching it a bank's vocabulary through a short instruction block works well for a handful of terms. Whether it holds up against a genuinely large, messy glossary is the open question.

Takeaway

A good assistant is defined by its no

It's easy to judge an assistant by its best answers. The more honest test is its worst case: what happens when someone asks for something it can't or shouldn't do. Getting that case right, clearly and without pretending, is most of what makes something feel trustworthy enough to actually rely on.
