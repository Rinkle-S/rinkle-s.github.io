Concept Boost

A Duolingo feature that turns a learner's repeated grammar mistakes into one concept to learn, practise and revisit.

The problem

Serious learners slide into streak-only sessions because grammar is taught one sentence at a time. Duolingo still counts them as healthy daily users.

The bet

Track the grammar concept a learner keeps missing, put one short checkpoint in their path, and check later that it stuck.

How it would be judged

Fewer repeat mistakes on a boosted concept after 2 weeks, against a matched group that got no boost.

This is a course project. I researched and designed it, and it has not been built or tested by Duolingo.

Phase 1 · Understand the product

What Duolingo is good at, and where it stops

I used Duolingo daily and tried nine flows, from daily lessons to reviewing mistakes. Short lessons, streaks and leaderboards make it easy to come back every day. That is why it keeps people better than Babbel or Busuu.

About 80% of its revenue comes from subscriptions, so the learners most likely to pay are the ones who want to get good at a language. For them, the gaps showed up quickly.

CompetitorDoes betterDoes worse
BabbelLinguist-designed lessons, explicit grammarPaid only, weaker habit, feels like a classroom
BusuuGrammar explanations, native-speaker correctionsSlow feedback, no habit loop
ChatGPT, YouTubeAnswers any doubt on demand, tailored practiceNo structure, no streak, can be wrong

I listed eleven user problems. Nine belonged to serious learners, and most were about missing explanations and feedback. My goal: help serious language learners understand and improve through better explanations, doubt resolution and actionable feedback.

Phase 2 · Research

Five interviews, thirty survey answers

I used interviews to understand why learners behave the way they do, and a survey to check how common it is. People in the survey placed themselves into Active or Maintenance-mode. I report every number against its own group, not against all 30.

SegmentSurveyWho they are
Maintenance-mode serious learners (primary)9 of 30Started to learn for real, now only keep the streak alive
Active serious learners (comparison)11 of 30Still learning properly, the group the fix should protect
Casual learnersOut of scopeCuriosity, low commitment

What came up

  • Grammar is the top confusion. 11 of 20 serious respondents named it, including 7 of 11 who are still active. All five interviewees raised it.
  • Help exists but goes unused. Explain My Answer is free for major languages. No interviewee used it. One tapped past it to keep going. Another skipped it, memorised the answer and moved on.
  • Learners beat the format. 15 of 20 used an outside resource in the last 3 months, and 7 of 20 used ChatGPT or another AI.
  • The streak replaces learning. 7 of 9 maintenance-mode respondents said most recent sessions were for the streak, against 2 of 11 active ones.

"I can often tell two options are wrong and the third is the obvious correct one, without necessarily understanding the full meaning. It becomes a pattern-matching game."

Interviewee, learning Japanese

"The original intent, learning something new, got replaced by streak psychology, which I only recognised after the fact."

Interviewee, learning German

One interviewee kept a streak alive for 3.5 years while her learning had effectively stopped. That case changed how I defined the segment. A streak can stay perfect while the learning is gone.

The P0 problem

Maintenance-mode serious learners meet grammar one sentence at a time. Nothing connects the pattern across lessons, so they keep getting marked correct without understanding why.

They guess through exercises, take their confusion to ChatGPT and YouTube, and shrink sessions to the shortest lesson that protects the streak. Duolingo keeps the streak and loses the learning, while the data still shows a healthy daily user.

Phase 3 · Find the answer

Why it happens, and what I considered

  1. Lessons are self-contained. The Unit Guidebook is thin, covers one unit, and sits behind a small icon.
  2. Nothing connects a concept across lessons. Explain My Answer covers one sentence at a time.
  3. Exercises can be passed by guessing or elimination.
  4. There is no cumulative place to review grammar across units.
  5. The streak treats every completed lesson as equal, so the shortest, most familiar lesson always wins.

I wrote 11 ideas, each tied to the causes it attacks, the one assumption it depends on, and a confidence label. I scored them with RICE, each factor from 1 to 3.

Showing 8 of 11. Three more low scorers were dropped: a cumulative grammar reference, split streak counters, and contrastive explanations.

Two ideas scored close to the top only because dividing by low effort rewards cheap ideas. Neither closes the problem alone. Idea 6 is the only one that connects mistakes across sessions, so I used it as the base and folded in the behaviour of the next two. I treated the score as an input, not the answer.

The solution

How Concept Boost works

It records which grammar concept each wrong answer belongs to. When the same concept is missed three times in a rolling window, a checkpoint appears in the learner's path. It does three things a plain correction cannot.

Names it

"You've missed this particle three times this week." Three scattered mistakes become one thing to fix.

Teaches and tests it

A one-line explanation with two examples, then a typed drill on unseen sentences. A wrong answer gets a different sentence, not the same one again.

Checks it stuck

Bonus XP, an automatic review about 3 days later, and a Grammar tab showing every concept as Still Weak, Practicing or Strong.

Decisions I made on purpose

  • Typed drill, not word bank. If a learner can pass by elimination, a correct answer proves nothing. The drill asks for the word plus its particle, not a full sentence, because one interviewee finds full sentences hard.
  • Two retries, then move on. After that the concept is restated plainly. The boost teaches, it never blocks the path.
  • No AI-written explanations in v1. A wrong grammar explanation costs more trust than none.
  • Not building: a chat tutor (overlaps Duolingo Max), a browsable grammar library (passive, so this group would not open it), or any change to the streak.

The PRD also covers five user stories with acceptance criteria, twelve edge cases and empty states, an event tracking sheet, dependencies, a timeline, and open questions.

Success and risk

How I would know it worked

TypeMetricWhy
KeyRepeat mistake rate on a boosted concept within 2 weeks, against a matched group with no boostTests whether the concept was learned, not whether the feature was used
SecondaryBoost adoption rate, then drill completion rateShows where people drop out before the key metric can move
DiagnosticFirst-attempt drill accuracy next to time on the explanation screenSeparates "did not read it" from "read it and it did not teach"
GuardrailLesson activity against each learner's own earlier baseline, drill abandonment, streak continuationThese learners already do the minimum. If the boost makes them do less, it failed

What could go wrong

AssumptionSignal to watchWhat I would do
Putting it in the path gets real engagement, not just a glanceFirst-attempt accuracy, read with explanation timeShorten the explanation to what people actually read. A gate can force a tap, not reading
The detector tells a real pattern from a typoExit rate on the boost intro screenLaunch on unambiguous concepts only, and hand-check the first 50 flags
Typed retries force real application without making people quitExit rate after the first wrong answerCap retries at 2 and tune from prototype data
Bonus XP can push comprehension, not just completionRepeat mistakes with and without the bonusShip it as a holdout. Drop it if the groups do not differ

Reflection

What I took from this

  • Usage data can look healthy while learning has stopped. The most useful finding came from one interview, not from a dashboard.
  • A score is a starting point. The highest RICE ideas were cheap, not complete, so I checked each one against the cause it was meant to fix.
  • A feature that learners skip needs a different design, not a better label. That moved the solution from "make help available" to "put it in the path and test it".
  • Open questions remain, including the right retry cap, whether the XP bonus should ship, and whether naming a repeated mistake could discourage some learners. A prototype test with real maintenance-mode learners is the next step.
